# Content pillars and story mining

Six pillars cover nearly every build-in-public post. Rotating between them keeps an account from becoming a stream of brag posts or a stream of complaints. This file covers how to find stories in raw material, then how to write each pillar.

## Story mining from raw material

Users paste messy things: "here's what happened this week", metric screenshots, customer emails, team chat logs. Your job is to find the stories in it, not to summarise it.

1. **Scan for signals:** numbers; surprises (expected X, got Y); decisions; a customer's exact words; mistakes; firsts; tension or stakes; things that took longer than expected; things the founder is unsure about.
2. **List 3 to 6 candidate stories.** For each: a one-line angle, the pillar, the concrete detail that carries it, and any flag (above their transparency level, needs consent, competitively sensitive).
3. **Rank** by specificity, tension, and how much the audience cares. A small story with a sharp detail beats a big story told vaguely.
4. **Act on the request:** if they asked for posts, draft the top one to three and show the rest as a list they can use later. If they asked "what can I post?", show the list and let them pick.

| Signal in the raw material | Likely pillar |
|---|---|
| "finally shipped", "first order", "hit 100" | Milestone |
| "we decided", "we debated", "we killed", "we chose" | Decision |
| "didn't work", "broke", "lost", "mistake", "wasted" | Failure retro |
| A customer message, review, call note or complaint | Customer story (consent needed) |
| Photos, a workspace, how something is made, a tool | Behind the scenes |
| "not sure whether", "can't decide", "what do you think" | Invitation |

**Chat logs and screenshots:** pull out the moment and the words, never the screenshot itself, unless everyone in it agrees. Strip names and identifying details by default.

**Questions for thin material** (ask up to three, pick the ones that matter):
- What exactly happened? Describe one moment.
- What number goes with it? How many, how long, how much, within your sharing level.
- What did the customer say, word for word?
- What did you expect, and what actually happened?
- What will you do differently?
- Is there a photo or screenshot you could share?

## 1. Milestones

**What:** first customer, first stranger, 100th order, a feature shipped, a launch, a year in business.
**Why it works:** progress markers let followers feel they're part of something moving.
**Structure:** the moment or number → what it took (the unseen part) → what's next or what it taught.
**By level:**
- L1: "First order from someone who isn't a friend or relative."
- L2: "100 users. 31% came from one comment I left in a forum."
- L3: "$1,200 MRR after 5 months. Here's where it comes from."

**Pitfall:** a milestone with no struggle reads as a brag. Pair it with what it cost or what nearly went wrong.

## 2. Decisions and trade-offs

**What:** something they chose and what they gave up: pricing, a feature cut, a supplier change, a hire, a market.
**Why it works:** it is the most useful pillar for other founders, it shows how the founder thinks, and it invites advice.
**Structure:** the choice → the options they considered → why this one → what they'll watch to know if it was right.
**By level:** works fully at L1 ("I chose to cut the free plan"). L2 and L3 can add the numbers behind the choice.
**Pitfall:** revealing competitive strategy. Run the competitor test from SKILL.md.

## 3. Failure retros

**What:** something that went wrong, why, and what changes now.
**Why it works:** honest failures are rare, so people notice and share them, and they build more trust than wins do.
**Structure:** what happened, specifically → what they expected → why it didn't work → what they're changing.
**Tone:** matter-of-fact. No self-punishment, and no tidy "failure is a gift" moral.
**By level:** L1 "Spent three weeks on a feature 2 people used." L3 "Spent $900 on ads that brought 4 sign-ups."
**Pitfalls:** blaming customers, staff or partners; posting about a problem that is still hurting current customers (fix it first, then tell the story).

## 4. Customer stories

**What:** a real customer's problem, their words, and what changed for them.
**Why it works:** it is proof that comes from someone other than the founder.
**Structure:** who (anonymised if needed) → what they were struggling with → their words → what changed.
**Rules:** consent before naming, quoting, or showing a screenshot. Never composite or invented customers. Paraphrase gets marked as paraphrase, not put in quotation marks.
**By level:** at L1, the story and the quote; at L2, add usage numbers; at L3, contract value only if the customer agrees.

## 5. Behind the scenes

**What:** how something is made, the workspace, tools, a day, packing orders, a messy whiteboard.
**Why it works:** it humanises the founder, is easy to make, and suits visual platforms.
**Structure:** one specific moment plus one detail most people wouldn't know ("each mug gets fired twice; the second firing is where most crack").
**Pitfall:** a pretty picture with no information. Every behind-the-scenes post should teach or reveal something.

## 6. Invitations to participate

**What:** asking the audience to vote, test, name something, give feedback, or join a beta.
**Why it works:** it turns followers into co-builders, which is the strongest driver of community.
**Structure:** context → one specific ask → what they'll do with the answers → when they'll report back. The context line carries the post's concrete detail: the number, quote or moment that made the founder ask ("62% of documents come back by email. Would a text push that higher?"). An invitation without one is just "thoughts?" with extra words.
**Rules:** only ask about things the founder will actually act on, and always close the loop with a follow-up post showing what happened. People stop answering when their input disappears.
**Details:** see `community-playbook.md` for question patterns and polls.
